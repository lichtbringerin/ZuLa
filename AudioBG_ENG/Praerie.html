    Station 7 – Praeries

    Textversion

    On your journey through the world of plants, you have now arrived at the vast grasslands of North America, the prairies. Originally, these treeless steppes stretched from the Rocky Mountains to the Great Lakes, covering around a third of the North American continent. Today, many of these areas have been replaced by agriculture, especially the tallgrass prairies, which have been almost completely converted into farmland. In the Wuerzburg Botanical Garden, you can see representatives of three classic prairie types. The shortgrass prairie, with smaller species adapted to drought. The mixed-grass prairie, as a transitional form, and the tallgrass prairie, with perennials up to three metres in height and rich in flowers. Typical for prairies are significant changes in appearance throughout the course of the year. The first flowers appear in late spring, with the peak following in summer and late summer, often lasting into November. The fact that North America had vast grasslands rather than dense forests, as we have in Central Europe, is only partly due to the climate, but mainly to two factors: regular fires, which were triggered in nature by lightning strikes or by humans. The flames burn quickly across the surface, but cause little damage to the grasses and shrubs that grow underground. The second factor was the presence of huge herds of bison, which roamed the plains and grazed the vegetation bare, keeping it short. This suppressed trees and promoted herbaceous perennials and, above all, grasses. In the Botanical Garden, the natural fire and the grazing herds of bison are imitated by annual mowing in February. Another highlight in this section are the hardy North American cacti that also grow here. They survive even deep frosts by drying out and shrinking in winter. However, some sensitive species must be protected from precipitation in winter with a foil roof. Take your time as you stroll through this often underestimated habitat full of diverse adaptations. Please note that the path is not paved, but leads over stones. A short climb takes you to a small hill from which you can enjoy a view of the North American prairie. If you visit several times throughout the year, you can see how the prairie changes constantly.
