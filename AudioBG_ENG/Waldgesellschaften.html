    Station 13 – Forests

    Textversion

    On your journey through the world of plants, this section now takes you to the forests of Central Europe. From shady beech forests to sparse pine forests. The different forest communities reflect the diversity of our native forests. Depending on the location, different forests develop with different types of trees, shrubs, herbs and mosses. A typical feature of forests is their so-called layered structure. This means that the plants grow in several layers on top of each other, from the tall tree crowns to the herb layer on the ground. The herb layer in particular changes significantly over the course of the year. If you have already visited the tropical house, you may have encountered this structure. Perhaps you will notice some differences? The forests in Germany are composed of various deciduous and coniferous tree species, with beech trees making up a very large proportion. They not only serve as a habitat for numerous animal species, but are also used for timber production. The timber production is important for the furniture industry, but also for firewood production. In recent years, however, many trees have died due to prolonged drought, heatwaves and pest infestation. The native beech tree (Fagus sylvatica) in particular is one of the losers of climate change. This is already visible to the naked eye in many regions. Here in the garden, too, there are gaps with dead wood. Now simply let yourself drift as you walk through the forest communities. The trail takes you through various Central European forests, from beech forests to dry pine forests. If you visit us again at a different time of year, you can observe how these forests change over the course of the year.
