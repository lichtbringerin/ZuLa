    Station 4 – Epiphytes

    Textversion

    In the lowland rainforest, there is fierce competition among plants for light. Sunlight is essential for photosynthesis, but due to the multi-layered structure and the dense canopy of the tall trees, only about 1% of the light reaches the ground. Some plants have developed special strategies to overcome this problem. These include so-called epiphytes, also known as tree-dwelling plants. These plants do not grow on the ground but on other trees. They use the height to be closer to the light. It is important, however, that they do not deprive their host of nutrients and merely use it as a location. However, since their roots do not reach the ground, epiphytes must absorb water and nutrients in other ways. A well-known example is bromeliads. Their leaves form funnelshaped structures in which rainwater collects, allowing them to absorb water and dissolved nutrients. This creates small water reservoirs that not only serve the plant itself but can also provide habitats for small animals. Many epiphytic orchids develop special aerial roots for water uptake. In addition to epiphytes, the rainforest features other adaptations to the lack of light. These include, for example, lianas that climb up trees, or strangler figs that encircle other trees with their trunks, thereby killing them and creating a clearing for themselves. If you look closely, you may discover yet another adaptation: some leaves are covered with small organisms such as mosses, algae, or lichens. These so-called epiphylls use the leaf surfaces as a habitat. For the plant itself, however, this can be disadvantageous, as it deprives the plant of additional light. To reduce the growth of such epiphytes, many rainforest plants have developed socalled drip tips. These elongated leaf tips ensure— —that water runs off quickly and the leaves dry rapidly, making the plant less susceptible to damage. As you walk through the forest, take note of the immense variety of plants in the rainforest.
