    Station 8 – Flower Gallery

    Textversion

    You are now in the so-called Flower Gallery. This narrow passageway showcases the diversity of flower shapes and colors. The plants here are regularly rotated, so there is something new to discover with every visit. But why do plants have flowers in the first place? They serve the purpose of reproduction, as they contain reproductive organs: the male stamens and the female carpels. Many flowers are hermaphroditic, meaning they bear both sexes in a single flower. To ensure pollination, plants rely on various strategies. Some are pollinated by the wind. Others rely on animals, primarily insects, but also birds or even mammals. To attract these pollinators, plants use striking colors, scents, or specific shapes. They often offer a reward in the form of nectar as well. Over the course of evolution, flowers and their pollinators have frequently evolved to be perfectly attuned to one another. If you look around here, you’ll notice the great variety of flowers. Some are large and striking, while others consist of many small individual flowers that together form an inflorescence. Take some time to look more closely at the different flower shapes—you might discover just how well they are adapted to their respective pollinators. In addition to the plants, you’ll also find a climate-controlled display case featuring tropical high-altitude plants, including some unusual carnivorous plant species. There are also aquariums with aquatic plants and various animals. The axolotls are particularly striking. These amphibians remain in the larval stage their entire lives yet still reach sexual maturity. This is known as paedomorphosis. Unlike many other amphibians, they do not undergo complete metamorphosis. What’s particularly fascinating is that if you inject an axolotl with the hormone thyroxine, it develops into a land-dwelling adult stage, similar to a salamander. In the wild, however, they lack this hormone and thus remain as larvae, just like our axolotls here in the Flower Walk of the Botanical Garden. In the wild, axolotls are found in only two lakes in Mexico and are now classified as critically endangered. Their name, “axolotl,” is Aztec and means something like “water monster.”
