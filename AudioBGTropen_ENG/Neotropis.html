    Station 7 – Neotropics

    Textversion

    On your journey through the dry tropics, you have now crossed continents. You are now in the Neotropics, that is, in the arid regions of Central and South America. At first glance, the plant life here appears very similar to that in the previous greenhouse section. But this impression is deceptive. For here, the true cacti grow. Even without flowers, cacti are easy to identify. An important identifying feature is their spines: their spines—usually numerous—are arranged in small, felt-like pads called areoles. This feature is characteristic of this plant family. In the Paläotropis, on the other hand, spurge plants often grow, whose thorns usually appear only in pairs or even singly. Due to their water-storing fleshy stems, these look very similar to cacti, but are not related to them. This phenomenon is called convergence. Here, different plants independently develop similar adaptations due to comparable environmental conditions. As in the previous station, the plants here face the challenge of getting by with little water. Many cacti are therefore succulents. They store water in their thickened stems and can thus survive long dry periods. You may notice the different growth forms of the cacti. Some are spherical, others grow columnar, or form flat shoots. These forms reflect different strategies. Spherical cacti have a particularly favorable volume-to-surface-area ratio. They can store a lot of water while losing very little of it. Columnar or ribbed cacti, on the other hand, increase their surface area to absorb more light for photosynthesis. The ribs can also cast shade, thereby protecting the plant from overheating. Other plants in this region also exhibit unique strategies. For example, you’ll find agaves here that store water in their leaves and simultaneously play an important role for humans as useful plants. Fibers or sweeteners are produced from many species. Agaves are particularly famous, however, for tequila, which is made from a specific species, Agave tequilana. Perhaps during the tour you will also recognize strategies you have already encountered at earlier stations. For example, pay attention to how plants store or collect water. Do you remember the bromeliads in the rainforest that store water in their leaves? This shows that plants, even in very different habitats, often develop similar solutions to the same challenges.
