    Station 6 – Palaeotropics

    Textversion

    At this station, our surroundings change significantly. After the humid tropics, you now enter the dry, arid regions of the tropics, the so-called Paleotropics—that is, Africa and Madagascar. The climate here is extreme, as rainfall is rare and irregular. Often, less than 250 millimeters falls per year. At the same time, solar radiation is very intense. During the day, temperatures can rise sharply to as high as 50 degrees, while at night they drop significantly, even to freezing temperatures. This aridity is closely linked to climatic processes at the equator. There, warm, humid air rises, cools, and drives the heavy rainfall in the rainforest. The now-dry air moves south and north toward the tropics, where it sinks again and heats up significantly. This creates stable high-pressure systems with dry climatic zones where rainfall is scarce. Plants here therefore face the challenge of getting by with very little water. Various strategies have developed over the course of evolution to cope with this. Some plants try to avoid dry periods. They germinate only after rare rains, grow quickly, flower, and produce seeds before it becomes dry again. Most of the time, they survive as seeds or in underground storage organs. You hardly see such plants here in the greenhouse, because the plants you see here mostly belong to the so-called xerophytes. They endure and tolerate the dryness and remain above ground even during long periods of drought. To conserve water, many of these plants have developed adaptations. These include small or greatly reduced leaves, thick waxy coatings, or dense hairiness that reduces evaporation. In some cases, the leaves have even transformed into thorns. Another strategy is water storage. So-called succulents store water in their fleshy leaves, stems, or roots and can thus survive prolonged dry spells. You may notice that some of the plants here resemble cacti. However, cacti are not native to Africa. In fact, these are plants from other families, such as the spurge family. Although they resemble cacti with their succulent stems, they are not closely related to them. As you walk through the exhibit, pay attention to these different adaptations, as they demonstrate just how diverse the strategies of plants are for surviving under extremely dry conditions.
