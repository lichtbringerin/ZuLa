    Station 2 – Lowland Rainforest

    Textversion

    Our journey through the Tropical House begins in the evergreen lowland rainforest. This term refers to the perpetually moist forests around the equator. They extend over a vast area up to about the 10th parallel, and in some cases far beyond. The largest contiguous areas are found in the South American Amazon Basin, but there are also intact lowland rainforests in Southeast Asia and Central Africa. These forests are among the most species-rich habitats on Earth, as 90% of all known animal and plant species call them home! The basis for this is the unique climate, as high precipitation and warm temperatures year-round result in very high humidity. In contrast to our climate in Central Europe, temperatures hardly change over the course of the year, and there are no distinct seasons. This is referred to as a socalled “tropical climate.” Plants grow under conditions that remain more or less constant year-round and therefore retain their leaves throughout the year. The forest is evergreen. If you take a look at the information boards (next to the audio station), you can compare the climate data for Würzburg with that of Manaus in the Amazon region. This clearly shows how constant the monthly temperatures (averaging over 25 °C) are there and how much higher the annual precipitation is. The cause of these conditions lies in the intense solar radiation at the equator. The warm air rises, absorbs large amounts of water, and cools again in higher atmospheric layers. Clouds form, from which heavy rain showers regularly develop. Here in the Tropical House, this climate is artificially created. During your tour, you may hear a loud hissing sound or notice a fine mist. These are our rain and fog machines, which are used to simulate rain and high humidity. Another characteristic of the rainforest is its so-called multi-layered structure. At the very top, individual giant trees tower up to 60 meters high. Below them, a dense canopy forms that allows very little light to reach the ground. It is dark on the ground. That is why there are so many climbing plants and vines here that grow rapidly upward to reach the light. Plants growing in the understory must be adapted to these low light conditions and often grow much more slowly. As a result, the herbaceous layer is also only sparsely developed. Feel free to take a closer look around this area and notice how differently the plants react to the competition for light.
